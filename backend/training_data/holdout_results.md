# Held-out evaluation: 12 team-scored essays

Generated by `scripts/evaluate_holdout.py` against the models currently in
`ml_models/` (trained on `sample_answers.json`, 76 examples). This script only
loads models; it never retrains and never touches `sample_answers.json`.
Re-running it produces identical numbers until the models are retrained.

**These 12 essays and their scores were written by the project team, not by
independent teachers.** This is a team-scored held-out check, not a measure of
inter-rater agreement with real graders. It tells us whether the model
generalises past the single-sentence training examples to paragraph-length
essays; it does not tell us whether the team's judgment matches a teacher's.

**12 essays is too few for QWK to be more than indicative.** Treat every
number below as a read on direction and rough size, not a precise estimate —
moving one essay's label by a point would visibly shift these numbers.

## Leakage guard

`evaluate_holdout.py` checks every holdout row against every training row for
the same criterion two ways: literal/near-literal text similarity, and cosine
similarity between frozen SBERT embeddings (the model's actual feature space).
Nothing crossed the duplicate threshold (text ratio ≥ 0.85 or cosine ≥ 0.90),
so the run went ahead.

Fifteen rows did cross a lower, non-blocking "notable" threshold (cosine ≥
0.60): the holdout essay reuses a real, well-known fact or argument shape that
a training example also used, in different wording. This is expected — these
are common facts about this essay prompt (the 2019 FTC/Facebook $5B
settlement, the EU Digital Services Act, "kids and social media") and a
team writing both training examples and holdout essays about the same prompt
will reach for the same handful of real facts. It is disclosed rather than
treated as disqualifying. Two are worth flagging by name because they bear on
how to read the results below:

- **Essay 1** (FTC/$5B) and **essay 11** (Cambridge Analytica, also citing the
  FTC/$5B fine) both echo training rows 51/72, which use the same fact. Essay
  1 scored a clean hit on both criteria; essay 11 did not (see below) — so the
  fact-overlap did not make essay 11 easier, but it may be inflating how good
  essay 1's result looks in isolation.
- **Essay 6** (EU Digital Services Act) echoes training row 22, which uses the
  same law. Essay 6 also scored close to true (Thesis −1, Evidence exact).

Full list of notable (non-blocking) matches is in the script's stdout; not
reproduced here to keep this file short.

Both criteria clear the guess-the-mean baseline by a wide margin on this set,
consistent with the leave-one-out numbers measured on the training data

larger in both places, which fits — it's the harder criterion to get right
from surface features.

The two worst misses are the same two essays on both criteria:

- **Essay 7** ("There are arguments on both sides... it is a hard question") —
  a genuinely fence-sitting essay with no real stance and no evidence. The
  model gave it a near-perfect Thesis score (5, true 1) and a mid Evidence
  score (4, true 0). It contains "while," which the reasoning-connective
  feature counts, and enough on-topic vocabulary ("regulation," "free
  speech," "protect users") to land near strong training examples in
  embedding space, even though the sentence commits to nothing.
- **Essay 11** (Cambridge Analytica/FTC facts, no argument connecting them) —
  the model gave it a maxed-out Evidence score (10, true 5) and an inflated
  Thesis score (4, true 1). This is the documented Evidence weakness
  ("rewards what evidence looks like, not whether it's relevant"), and this
  run shows it is not confined to the Evidence score: because production
  feeds the *same full essay text* to every criterion's model (see
  `app/grading/engine.py`), a fact-heavy paragraph with no real thesis
  inflates the Thesis score too. That's a new finding — the earlier
  single-sentence training/testing couldn't have shown it, because a Thesis
  example and an Evidence example were never the same text.

By contrast, **essay 4** — also fact-heavy with no real argument, the other
"known weakness" essay — scored exactly right on both criteria (Thesis 1/1,
Evidence 1/1). The difference looks like the specific facts involved: essay
4's facts are a generic list of platforms/countries/years, while essay 11's
are a named scandal with a dollar figure and a year (`$5 billion`, `2019`,
`FTC`) — a pattern that appears, worded differently, in several *high*-scoring
training examples (rows 51, 72). So the failure is not just "facts without
argument"; it's specifically facts that resemble a strong-evidence pattern
already in training, restated without the argument that would justify the
strong score.

## Does essay length explain the errors?

| | Thesis MAE | Evidence MAE |
|---|---|---|
| paragraph-length (1, 2, 6, 8, 10, 12) | 0.67 | 1.50 |
| short (3, 4, 5, 7, 9, 11) | 1.33 | 1.83 |

Word counts confirm the two groups really do differ in length (51–64 words
for the "long" essays, 17–47 for the "short" ones), so this isn't just a
labeling artifact. On the surface, short essays have the larger error on both
criteria.

**But the gap doesn't hold up as a length effect once you look inside each
group.** Four of the six short essays (3, 4, 5, 9) scored a perfect 0 error
on Thesis, and three (3, 4, 5) on Evidence too. All of the short group's
error is concentrated in exactly two essays — 7 and 11 — the same two
discussed above. Remove those two and the short group's MAE drops to 0 on
both criteria, well *below* the long group's. Symmetrically, the long group's
Evidence error is concentrated in essays 10 and 12, not spread across all six.

So: **the errors are explained by content, not length.** Essays 7 and 11 fail
because of the specific patterns above (fence-sitting; facts resembling a
strong-evidence template but not tied to the argument), not because they are
short. It's a coincidence of this particular set that both landed in the
"short" bucket — length and "facts untied to argument" happen to be
correlated in this sample of 12, but nothing in the failure mode is actually
about word count.

## Bottom line

The model clears its own guess-the-mean baseline comfortably on essays it has
never seen, in a format (full paragraphs) it wasn't trained on — that's a
real, useful signal that it generalises past single-sentence training
examples. The known Evidence weakness is confirmed and sharpened: it isn't
just "rewards fact-shaped text," it specifically over-rewards facts that
resemble an already-seen strong-evidence pattern, and — new in this run — that
inflation carries into the Thesis score too, because both criteria score the
same submitted text. Teacher review remains the backstop for exactly this
kind of case.
